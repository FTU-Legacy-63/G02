## 8. Difficulties Encountered and How I Handled Them

### (1) Separating historical information from fictional information

- **Problem:** The case is inspired by Toys "R" Us, but Toy Kingdom, the Anderson family, Houndstooth Capital and many other events are fictional. Without clear separation, viewers could easily mistake them for historical fact or use a source to support a conclusion stronger than the evidence allows.
- **Solution:** Established an S / A / G coding system, clearly recording which type each piece of information belongs to (Historical, Historical / Adapted, Simulated, Game State), and stating the limits and disclosure method of each entry in Assumptions and the Source Use Map.

### (2) Balancing "giving information" with "keeping the challenge"

- **Problem:** An important detail (such as the EBITDA threshold of the Houndstooth agreement) was revealed too early in a follow-up question, making the full opening of HS-G almost worthless and weakening the information trade-off mechanism between screens.
- **Solution:** Reworded the answer to state only that a conditional agreement affecting ownership exists, while the specific details must be found in the file. I did not change the question's position or the number of options.

### (3) Keeping questions open without an implicitly closed answer

- **Problem:** The names of the diagnoses could implicitly tell the player which is the root cause, while the character David says he does not know where the problem lies.
- **Solution:** Renamed the displayed labels using a "problem layers" framework, added a short explanatory paragraph, and kept the codes, number of options, evidence columns and scoring rules unchanged.

### (4) Ensuring Screen 10 consequences support the reassessment at Screen 11

- **Problem:** If a player makes a wrong diagnosis and no event at Screen 10 hints at it, Screen 11 becomes mere guesswork rather than updating a judgment based on new data.
- **Solution:** Added consequence logic lines within the existing tabs, so that every wrong diagnosis has at least one directly contradicting event, and parties such as employees, customers and landlords also have observable consequences. The screen structure remained unchanged.

### (5) Coordinating under limited scope and time

- **Problem:** Some team feedback required structural changes (adding a step to re-propose the treatment, reducing the number of files or questions, splitting Screen 8 into two screens), which would affect the Code and UI-UX teams and might miss the deadline.
- **Solution:** Split the feedback into two groups: content edits (applied immediately) and structural changes (left for the team to decide separately), which kept the completed work stable.

## 9. A Message to Future Students

In a simulation project, a good story is not enough: every detail in the scenario needs a reason to exist, must be connected to a player decision, and must have a clear source or assumption. Build the habit of recording where you got information and what you created yourself, because when you are questioned, that is what lets you defend your product.

When working in a team, always ask *"Does this edit change anyone else's work?"* Many problems can be solved just by changing wording without changing the structure, and major changes should be decided by the whole team.

Finally, don't be afraid to cut: content that doesn't serve the player's decisions should be removed, so that what remains is clear and carries weight.
